The Situation — What Is Actually Happening

A larger industrial firm (Cooper) wants control of an under-performing tool maker (Nicholson) by swapping stock, not paying cash. A rival bidder (VLN) is circling, and Nicholson's shares are split across groups who each want something different.

0

Recognise the case type first

Before any number, classify the problem — it tells you which tools to reach for. Getting this right in the first 30 seconds is worth marks.

$44Nicholson mkt price $24Cooper share price 584kNicholson shares 4.22mCooper shares
Signals it's a Cooper-type case
  • Buyer pays with its own shares (stock swap / exchange offer), not cash.
  • The ask is a swap ratio — Cooper shares per Nicholson share.
  • Different shareholder groups (family, promoters, speculators).
  • Worry about diluting EPS or book value after the deal.
Don't confuse it with
  • Kohler — valuing a private firm for a fair-value lawsuit (minority & illiquidity discounts).
  • Stanley B&D — do synergies beat the premium (accretion timing).
  • Different question → different toolkit.
The whole case in one lineOffer too few Cooper shares and Nicholson's holders reject it; offer too many and Cooper dilutes its own shareholders. The right swap ratio must satisfy both sides at once — and the four lenses below bracket where that number lives.

The Four Lenses — Worked by Hand

Each lens gives a swap ratio (or a value that implies one). Compute all four, then triangulate. Every number below is reproducible with a calculator and matched to the workbook.

1

Method 1 · DCF — what Nicholson is really worth

Ignore the noisy market price; value the cash the business will generate. Project 10 years (1972–1981), convert profit to Free Cash Flow to Equity, discount at the cost of equity, add a terminal value. This gives Cooper a defensible floor.

Why FCFE here (not FCFF)?

The solution builds cash flow to equity holders (after interest), so it must be discounted at the cost of equity (Ke), not WACC. Matching the cash flow to the right discount rate is the number-one place students lose marks.

  1. Project revenue at 6% growth
    Revenue1971 = 55.3 // last actual
    Revenuet = prior × 1.06 → 58.6, 62.1, 65.9 … 99.0 by 1981
  2. Subtract costs to get operating profit (NOP)
    NOP = Revenue − COGS − SG&A − Dep − Other
    // COGS improves 69%→65% of sales; SG&A 22%→19%; Dep $2.1 fixed; Other $0.2
    NOP1972 = 4.73
  3. Tax at 40% → profit after tax (PAT)
    Tax = 40% × (NOP − Interest 0.8)
    PAT = NOP − Tax → PAT1972 = 3.16
  4. Convert PAT to cash — the FCFE build
    FCFE = PAT + Dep − ΔWorking Capital − Capex
    // add Dep back (non-cash); subtract cash tied up in WC as sales grow; subtract equipment spend
    FCFE1972 = 3.16 + 2.1 − 1.44 − 2.1 = 1.72
    ⚠ Profit is not cashIf you skip this step and discount PAT, you value the wrong thing. Always add depreciation, subtract the working-capital increase, subtract capex.
  5. Terminal value (perpetuity)
    TV = final-year FCFE ÷ Ke = 6.01 ÷ 0.10 ≈ 60.1
    // captures all cash beyond 1981; then discount it back like any other year
  6. Discount every year at Ke ≈ 10% and sum
    PV factort = 1 / (1.10)t → 0.909, 0.826, 0.751 …
    Equity value = Σ (FCFEt × factort) + PV(TV) = $47.1m
    Value / share = 47.1m × 106 ÷ 584,000 = $80.6

1b

The discount rate — cost of equity via the Dividend Discount Model

Because we discounted cash to equity, the rate is Ke. The solution derives it from the Gordon (dividend growth) model rather than CAPM.

  1. The Gordon model, rearranged for return
    Price P = D1 / (Ke − g) ⇒ Ke = D1/P + g
    // D1 = next year's dividend = D0 × (1+g)
  2. Plug in Nicholson's numbers
    D0 = 1.4 ; g = 5% ; P = 27.5
    Ke = (1.4 × 1.05) / 27.5 + 0.05 = 0.0535 + 0.05 = 10.35%
  3. Round for discounting
    Ke ≈ 10% // used as the PV rate and in the terminal value
⚠ Small inconsistency to flagThe workbook uses P = 27.5 in the Ke formula but lists the average price as $28 just beside it. It rounds to ~10% either way, so the answer doesn't move — but show the D1/P + g working so the grader sees exactly where 10% came from.
Why DDM instead of CAPM hereDDM needs only a dividend, a growth rate, and a price — all directly available. CAPM would need a beta from comparable public companies. Either is defensible; state which you used and why.

Method 2 · EPS-based swap — the heart of the case

Cooper pays with its own shares. Every new share splits Cooper's profit among more shares — which can cut its earnings per share. So Cooper asks: what is the most shares I can issue before my EPS falls? That maximum is the safe ceiling.

The breakeven logic (do this on paper)

For Cooper's EPS to stay unchanged, the combined profit can be spread over no more than (Combined PAT ÷ Cooper's old EPS) shares. The gap to Cooper's existing count is the maximum new shares → the maximum swap ratio.

Cooper EPS = own PAT ÷ own shares
Max total shares = Combined PAT ÷ Cooper EPS
Max new shares = Max total − Cooper existing
Swap ratio = Max new shares ÷ Nicholson shares
Value offered = Swap ratio × Cooper price

Worked by hand — 1972

Cooper EPS (1972) = 11.0 / 4.22 = 2.607 ← must not fall below
Combined PAT = 11.0 + 3.16 = 14.16 (Cooper + Nicholson's projected PAT)
Max total shares = 14.16 / 2.607 = 5.43
Max new shares = 5.43 − 4.22 = 1.21
Swap ratio = 1.21 / 0.584 = 2.08 Cooper shares per Nicholson share
Value offered = 2.08 × $24 = $49.8 vs Nicholson market $44 → +13–24% premium

⚠ Three ways students get this wrong (1) Use Cooper's EPS as the floor (the acquirer's), not Nicholson's — mixing them flips the answer.

(3) Memorise the chain: combined PAT ÷ old EPS = max total shares. Everything else follows.

Method 3 · Book-value swap — the cross-check

Book value = accounting net worth (assets − liabilities). Swapping in proportion to book value per share is a simple, objective cross-check — especially useful when the target's real assets are worth more than the books show.

BV / Nicholson share = 31,000,000 / 584,000 = $53.08
BV / Cooper share = 85,000,000 / 4.22m = $20.15
Swap ratio (raw) = 53.08 / 20.15 = 2.63

Revalue Nicholson's real assets (+$9.2m understated on the books):
Adjusted BV / Nich. share = (31.0 + 9.2)m / 584,000 = $68.84
Adjusted swap ratio = 68.84 / 20.15 = 3.42

Method 4 · Shareholder-group analysis & deal design

The maths sets the range; the people decide the structure. Identify who holds the swing votes and what instrument satisfies conflicting wants at once.

The conflict to resolveSpeculators want cash now. The family wants control, income, security, and no tax bill. A single plain-vanilla offer can't satisfy both — so the winning move is a cleverly designed instrument, not just a number.
★ The design insight (why a convertible preferred wins)The structure used a convertible preferred share paying a $2.50 dividend, convertible into ~1.8–2.0 Cooper shares. It threads every needle at once:
• Income + security for the family (the fixed preferred dividend)
• Upside if Cooper rises (convert into common)
• Tax efficiency (a share-for-share exchange defers tax vs a cash sale)
• EPS protection for Cooper (preferred isn't common until converted)

Q · What swap ratio should Cooper offer, and why?

Roughly 2 to 2.5 Cooper shares per Nicholson share. It sits below the EPS ceiling (so Cooper isn't diluted), above a fair premium on Nicholson's $44 price (so holders say yes), and is well under the DCF worth of ~$80 (so Cooper still captures value). Deliver it as a convertible preferred to win the family.

Q · Why not just offer the DCF value of $80?

Because paying $80 in Cooper stock would force Cooper to issue so many shares that its own EPS falls sharply (well past the ~2.9 ceiling). The DCF tells you Nicholson is worth a lot; the EPS test tells you what Cooper can afford to pay without hurting its own shareholders. The answer lives where they overlap.

Q · What actually happened?

Cooper succeeded. It quietly bought up to ~22% of Nicholson in the open market (~$6.2m), then offered the rest a convertible preferred paying a $2.50 dividend, convertible into ~1.8–2.0 Cooper shares. It satisfied the family's need for income and security while protecting Cooper's EPS. Cooper's stock later hit $44 in 1973 as earnings rose — the deal created value.

Exam drill & self-test — cover the answers

Solve any Cooper-type case in 7 moves

  1. Identify the type
    Stock-swap acquisition + "swap ratio" asked + shareholder groups = Cooper-type. Reach for the four lenses.
  2. DCF the target
    Revenue → costs → PAT → convert to FCFE (add dep, −ΔWC, −capex) → discount at Ke → add TV → ÷ shares. Intrinsic floor.
  3. EPS ceiling
    Max total shares = combined PAT ÷ acquirer's old EPS. Minus existing = max new. ÷ target shares = max swap ratio.
  4. Book-value cross-check
    BV/share target ÷ BV/share acquirer = swap ratio. Revalue hidden assets if told to.
  5. Group analysis
    Tabulate each group: shares, motive, what wins them. Find who holds the swing votes (here, the family).
  6. Triangulate
    Overlay the ranges. Pick a ratio below the EPS ceiling yet above a fair premium on market price.
  7. Design the instrument
    If groups conflict, propose a convertible preferred (income + upside + tax efficiency) to satisfy everyone at once.
What are the four lenses and what does each set?
DCF = intrinsic floor (~$80.6). EPS swap = ceiling for the acquirer (2.1–2.9). Book value = upper cross-check (2.6–3.4). Group analysis = the instrument that wins the family.
Write the DCF value-per-share chain with numbers.
Equity value = Σ(FCFE×PV factor) + PV(TV) = $47.1m. ÷ 584,000 shares × 106 = ~$80.6/share. Ke = 10% (DDM); TV = final FCFE ÷ 0.10.
Why FCFE and not FCFF in this DCF?
The cash flow is built after interest (to equity holders), so it must be discounted at the cost of equity, not WACC. Match the cash flow to the matching rate.
Give the EPS breakeven formula.
Max total shares = Combined PAT ÷ acquirer's standalone EPS; swap ratio = (max total − acquirer existing) ÷ target shares.
1972 EPS swap — run it.
Cooper EPS 11/4.22 = 2.607. Combined PAT 14.16. Max total 14.16/2.607 = 5.43. Max new 1.21. Swap 1.21/0.584 = 2.08. Offer 2.08×$24 = $49.8 (vs $44 market).
Why is the EPS swap ratio a range, not one number?
Nicholson's profits grow each year, so Cooper can safely issue more shares over time — the ceiling rises from ~2.1 (1972) to ~2.9 (1976) and settles there.
Book-value swap before and after the asset revaluation?
Before: 53.08/20.15 = 2.63. After adding $9.2m of understated assets: 68.84/20.15 = 3.42 (86% premium).
Cost of equity — which model, and the numbers?
Dividend Discount (Gordon): Ke = D1/P + g = (1.4×1.05)/27.5 + 0.05 = 10.35% ≈ 10%. Flag the $27.5 vs $28 price inconsistency.
Who holds the swing votes, and what instrument won them?
The family/promoters. A convertible preferred ($2.50 dividend, convertible into ~1.8–2.0 Cooper shares) gave income + security + upside + tax deferral — satisfying conflicting groups at once.
Why not offer the full DCF value of ~$80?
Paying $80 in stock forces so many new Cooper shares that Cooper's own EPS falls past the ~2.9 ceiling. DCF = what Nicholson is worth; EPS test = what Cooper can afford. Answer sits in the overlap (~2.0–2.5).
The one-line memory hook.
